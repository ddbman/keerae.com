Squish n Pops

The free demo’s out. Ten levels on itch.io, and the rest of the 400 are on the way.

Hold your phone sideways. Tilt to steer, tap to fire. Every hit splits a bubble into two smaller ones, so a good shot basically hands you two new problems. Clear the screen and you’re out.

The demo’s out. Ten levels, free, no ads.

The first seven teach you the basics. The last three skip ahead to where the weird bubbles start showing up. It runs right in your phone browser. No account, nothing to install.

Play it on itch.io

This is SHORT FUSE, the last one in the demo. Set one bomb off and the chain pretty much plays the level for you.

Nothing in this game ever speeds up. A bubble in level 200 moves at exactly the same speed as one in level 1, and each size always bounces back to the same height. All the difficulty is in how many there are, what they do, when they show up and how the room is shaped.

iOS will only hand a web page the tilt sensors if you ask from inside a real tap, and only over HTTPS. So if someone says no, the game just flips to touch and lets them keep playing.

Into Ember Depths

In development. Desktop and browser.

An online bullet-hell action RPG. You start out in a daylight town and fight your way inward through rings that keep getting darker, then head down into a descent that doesn’t end. Every floor’s boss opens the next one.

Every boss in the game, in no particular order. Seven up top, six in the dungeons, twenty down in the descent. Not a single palette swap.

The desktop build and the browser build run the exact same code now, and they play on the same server. I cut every cooldown so abilities come out of MP, tightened the aggro range, and gave the two easiest rings four map bosses each instead of one.

Rattlecup

Vertical slice, building for iOS and Android. Working title.

A liar’s dice roguelike. One rule does all the heavy lifting here. Are they telling the truth, or do I call them a liar? That question carries a whole run. Three floors of a crooked gambling house, your dice are your build, and every claim that misses gets paid out of your own health.

The art direction is called The Vigil. A candle, some faces, nothing else. Warm gold nearly everywhere, and red pretty much just means damage or the call.

Special dice and charms run on one effect system. Tricks and table modifiers are basically just data. So a new item is usually a line in a file, not new code. The smarter opponents read a public tell ledger and price you into it, which is still my favourite thing in there.

Yeah, it’s all me.

Four codebases and two engines. Into Ember Depths doesn’t even use an engine. Here’s what each tool is actually doing.

ToolWhereWhat I’d tell you
TypeScript + Phaser 3Squish n PopsThe game logic never touches the artwork directly, so I can swap out the sprite art without changing any game code.
ElectronInto Ember DepthsIt wraps the same code as the browser build, so one codebase ships to both. For an action game that costs you bundle size and memory. I took the trade anyway.
Canvas2D, no build stepInto Ember DepthsPlain ES modules, served exactly as they are. Nothing to compile means nothing sitting between making a change and seeing it.
Node + WebSocketsInto Ember DepthsOne server, and any mix of desktop and browser players on the same wire.
Godot 4 + GDScriptRattlecup, RollawayPicked it for the scene and component model. Both games are on 4.7 now, and neither one moved until its whole test suite came back green.
Headless test suitesAll of themThe game rules all get tested without a renderer. That’s the only reason I can make balance changes fast and still trust them.
